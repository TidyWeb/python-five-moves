The Five Moves

One method. Twenty scenarios that share nothing on the surface. Repetition until the scenario stops mattering and the skeleton is all you see.

00

Why this document exists

You can already follow a decision problem when someone walks you through it. What you cannot yet do is meet a new one cold and know where to put your hands.

That is not a knowledge gap. It is a rehearsal gap. Understanding arrives the first time; it just doesn't stay unless you make it come back.

In plain english

One run through a method is something you did. Ten runs is something you know. Nothing else closes that gap — not better notes, not a cleverer explanation, not more time staring at it.

So this pack contains exactly one method and twenty scenarios to run it against. The method never changes. The scenarios are deliberately unalike — different worlds, different words, different numbers. That contrast is the point. You are training yourself to see the same skeleton under different meat.

Two rules while you work

Rule

No code until the sheet is finished. Not one line. If you catch yourself typing if, you have skipped ahead — go back.

Do the sheet by hand, from memory, every time. Even when it feels pointless. Especially then. The tenth time you write "Carrier:" without thinking about it is the moment it has landed.

01

First, something to hold

A method explained in the abstract slides straight off. You cannot think about "the final result" until you have a task in front of you that has one.

So before the five moves are described, here is a very small scenario. It stays with you through every one of them. Each move gets stated, and then immediately applied to this — so you always have something concrete in your hand when the question arrives.

It is deliberately tiny. Small enough to hold in your head without effort, which means all your attention can go on the method rather than on the story.

The running scenario · the coffee cart

Ravi runs a coffee cart outside the station.

He sells filter coffee: £2.20 for a small, £2.80 for a large.

He also sells speciality coffee: £3.10 for a small, £3.90 for a large.

Customers with a loyalty card get 50p off whatever their drink costs.

Ravi needs one amount to charge.

That is the whole thing. Four prices, one discount. Keep it in mind as you read the next section — every move will be applied to it in an orange panel immediately after the move is explained.

In plain english

You are about to learn five questions. Questions need something to be about.

The coffee cart is that something. It is not the lesson — it is the thing the lesson gets done to.

02

The five moves

Scene · Clues · Carrier · Order · Silhouette Say these until they arrive without effort. Everything else hangs off them.

Each move below has a question it asks, and an output it produces. The output matters. A move you cannot show the result of is a move you haven't actually done.

After each move is explained, an orange panel shows that move carried out on Ravi's coffee cart. Read the move, then read the panel, then move on. By the end of this section the cart will be a finished program.

MOVE 01 SCENE

What single result does someone actually need, and what are the big parts?

Before anything else, finish this sentence:

Finish the sentence

At the end, one ______________ must exist.

A price. A yes or no. A category name. A message. A count.

If you cannot finish that sentence, you do not yet understand the task, and no amount of if statements will rescue you.

Then ask whether the job splits into parts that happen in sequence — where the second part cannot begin until the first has produced something. Most real tasks do.

Output of this move

One sentence naming the final result, plus a list of the major parts in the order they must happen.

Move 1 on the coffee cart

At the end, one drink price in pounds must exist.

Ravi does not need a list of prices, or a description, or a category. He needs a single number to ask the customer for.

Does it split into parts that happen in sequence? Yes, two:

  • Part A — work out the basic drink price.
  • Part B — take 50p off if they have a loyalty card.

Part B cannot start until Part A has produced a price. There is nothing to take 50p from otherwise. Sequence confirmed.

MOVE 02 CLUES

What can vary, how many states does each fact have, and where are the boundaries?

Read the scenario twice. First for the story. Second with a pen, marking every fact that could be different for the next customer, patient, order or pupil.

For each one, write three things down.

NameWhat you will call the variable
StatesHow many different values matter, and what they are
JobWhat this fact actually decides

Counting the states is not bookkeeping. It is your branch count. If a fact has three states, you owe the program three branches — and you will know instantly if you have written only two.

Two things to hunt for on purpose

  • Noise. Facts mentioned in the scenario that cannot change the answer. The customer's name. The street the shop is on. Cross them out deliberately — the act of dismissing a fact is itself a skill, and it is one you have to practise.
  • Boundaries. "Under 2 kg" and "2 kg or more" is a boundary. Where exactly does one state end and the next begin — and which side does the boundary value itself sit on?
Trap

Boundaries are where most wrong answers come from, and they fail quietly. A program that charges the wrong amount only at exactly 2.0 kg will pass every test you casually throw at it.

Write the boundary down in words before you write it in code. "Under 2" and "2 or more" is a decision you make once, on paper, not a thing you improvise at the keyboard.

Output of this move

A table of facts with their state counts and boundaries, a list of the noise you dismissed, and a total branch count.

Move 2 on the coffee cart

Reading Ravi's scenario again with a pen, three facts can change from customer to customer.

Noise: Ravi's name, the station, whether it is takeaway. None of them can move the number, so they are crossed out on purpose.

Branch count for Part A: 2 types × 2 sizes = four possible basic prices. And there are indeed exactly four prices in the scenario. The count checks out.

Part B is a separate two-state decision applied afterwards. It does not multiply into Part A — there are not eight routes. That single distinction is the reason Part B ends up outside the nest rather than inside it.

Note there are no numeric boundaries here. Nothing is "under" or "or more". That makes this scenario an easy one — and it is exactly why the drills add boundaries later.

MOVE 03 CARRIER

What one named value travels from part to part?

This is the move most beginners never make, and it is the one that turns a loose pile of conditions into a machine.

Somewhere in the task there is a value that is unknown before the hard part runs and known after it. Every route through the hard part must end by putting something into it. Everything downstream reads from it without caring which route filled it.

In plain english

It is one labelled box, not one box per outcome.

Six possible prices do not mean six variables. They mean six routes that all end up writing into the same box. The next part of the program opens that box and finds one number, and it neither knows nor cares how the number got there.

A useful way to find it: ask yourself, if I stopped the program halfway, what would I need to be holding in order to carry on? That thing is your carrier.

Name it now, name it well, and never rename it.

Carriers come in three shapes

  • The single value. Unknown, then set exactly once. A price, a band name, a decision. Most tasks.
  • The accumulator. Starts at zero or empty, and grows as the program runs. Running totals, counts, tiered bills.
  • The pair. Two things must travel together — an amount and a reason, a band and a flag. Rarer, but real.
Output of this move

The carrier's name, its type, its shape, and what it holds before and after the hard part.

Move 3 on the coffee cart

The carrier is drink_price.

Namedrink_price

After Part Aholds exactly one of 2.20, 2.80, 3.10, 3.90

Apply the test: if you stopped Ravi's program halfway, what would you need to be holding to carry on? A price. That is the carrier.

And note what it is not. It is not four variables called filter_small, filter_large, spec_small, spec_large. Four routes lead into one box. The loyalty check that follows does not know or care which route filled it — it just finds a number there and takes 50p off.

MOVE 04 ORDER

Which question picks the rulebook? Which only mean something once it is open? Does everything rejoin?

Three sub-questions, always taken in this order.

a) What is the outer question?

Not the most obvious fact. Not the first one mentioned. The one that decides which set of rules applies.

The test

If you did not know the answer to this question, would the other facts still mean anything?

If the answer is no — the other facts go inert without it — you have found your outer question.

b) What is genuinely inside?

A question is nested when it is only meaningful after another has been answered. Weight bands mean nothing until you know which price list you're reading. Fine rates mean nothing until you've established the item is overdue.

A question is not nested when it applies regardless of route. Those go afterwards, at the far-left margin, asked once.

Trap

The temptation is to put the later question inside every route, because that's the order you thought of it in.

Resist it. Six copies of the same discount rule is six chances to get it wrong, and six places to edit when the rate changes. Wait for the rejoin. Ask once.

c) Does every route rejoin?

Walk each path to its end. Does it fill the carrier?

If any path can finish without doing so, you have a hole. It will bite you later as a NameError, or worse, as a silently wrong answer using a value left over from something else.

Output of this move

The outer question, the inner questions, the later questions that apply to everyone, and a confirmation that every single route fills the carrier.

Move 4 on the coffee cart

Outer question: is it a speciality coffee?

Apply the test from part (a). If you did not know whether it was filter or speciality, would cup_size still mean anything?

No. "Large" on its own is not a price. It could be £2.80 or £3.90, and there is no way to choose between them. The size goes inert without the type. That is the outer question found.

Inner question: is it a small cup? Meaningful only once a price pair has been opened. Inside filter, small means £2.20. Inside speciality, small means £3.10. Same question, two different answers, depending on which world you are standing in.

Later, for everyone: do they have a loyalty card? This one does not care which of the four routes was taken. 50p comes off a filter small exactly as it comes off a speciality large. So it waits at the far-left margin, after the rejoin, and gets asked once.

Every route fills the carrier? Yes — all four end in an assignment to drink_price. Walk them: filter-small, filter-large, speciality-small, speciality-large. Four routes, four assignments, no holes.

MOVE 05 SILHOUETTE

Sketch the shape in half-English before you write real syntax.

Write the structure out with proper indentation, but with plain words where the conditions will go.

if <outer question>:
    if <inner question 1>:
        carrier = ...
    elif <inner question 2>:
        carrier = ...
    else:
        carrier = ...
else:
    if <inner question 1>:
        carrier = ...
    ...

if <the later question, applying to everyone>:
    carrier = adjusted

The colours track the layers: orange is the outer layer, blue is the middle, green is the innermost work.

You are checking two things here. That the nesting depth is right, and that the returns to the far-left margin happen where they should.

In plain english

Indentation is not decoration. It is a record of which question you are currently inside.

Coming back out to the margin is the code making a statement: that whole investigation is finished, and what follows applies to everybody, whichever route they took to get here.

Output of this move

A sketched shape, correctly indented, with English in the condition slots. Only now do you write Python.

Move 5 on the coffee cart

The shape, in half-English:

if <speciality coffee>:
    if <small cup>:
        drink_price = ...
    else:
        drink_price = ...
else:
    if <small cup>:
        drink_price = ...
    else:
        drink_price = ...

    drink_price = 50p less

Two checks. Is the nesting depth right? Yes — two levels in Part A, because two questions had to be answered in order. Does the return to the far-left margin happen in the right place? Yes — after all four routes have finished, not inside any of them.

The sheet is now complete. Only at this point does Python appear.

The coffee cart, finished

Five moves done, so the code is now a translation job rather than a thinking job. There is nothing left to decide.

# --- Facts ---
coffee_type = "speciality"
cup_size = "large"
has_loyalty_card = True


# --- Part A: find the basic drink price ---
# Outer question: which price pair applies?
if coffee_type == "speciality":

    # Inner question: which size, within the speciality pair?
    if cup_size == "small":
        drink_price = 3.10
    else:
        drink_price = 3.90

else:

    # Inner question: which size, within the filter pair?
    if cup_size == "small":
        drink_price = 2.20
    else:
        drink_price = 2.80


# All four routes have rejoined.
# drink_price holds exactly one value.


# --- Part B: apply the loyalty discount ---
# Far-left margin: applies to everyone, whatever route they took.
if has_loyalty_card:
    drink_price = drink_price - 0.50


# --- Output ---
print(f"That'll be £{drink_price:.2f} please")

Output:

That'll be £3.40 please
What just happened

You did not decide anything while writing that code. Every decision had already been made on the sheet — which question goes outside, which goes inside, what the box is called, where the margin returns.

That is the entire purpose of the five moves. Writing code should be the boring part. If it feels like the hard part, it means the thinking has not been done yet.

04

The syntax spine

Colons and f-strings are the two things you named as a nightmare. Here is the whole of what you need, in one place — and then it appears in every single scenario until it becomes boring.

The colon rule

Rule

A colon means: what follows is indented, and belongs to me.

Every line that opens a block ends in a colon. That is if, elif, else, for, while, def. No exceptions anywhere in the language.

if weight < 2:          # colon — opens a block
    charge = 8          # indented, belongs to the if
elif weight < 10:       # colon
    charge = 12
else:                   # colon, even with no condition
    charge = 18

else takes a colon even though it asks nothing at all. It still opens a block, so it still gets one.

Lines that do not open a block do not get a colon. charge = 8 has none. print(charge) has none.

Quick self-test

Does the next line need to be indented?

If yes — colon. If no — no colon. That is the entire rule, and it never has an exception.

f-strings

An f-string lets you drop a variable's value into a piece of text.

Put an f immediately before the opening quote. Put the variable inside curly braces within the text.

name = "Maya"
charge = 12

print(f"Hello {name}, your charge is {charge}")

Output:

Hello Maya, your charge is 12
Trap

Without the f, the braces stay as literal text and nothing gets substituted. This is the single most common f-string mistake, and it fails silently — you get output, it's just wrong.

print("Hello {name}")     # prints:  Hello {name}
print(f"Hello {name}")    # prints:  Hello Maya

You can put a calculation inside the braces as well, not just a bare variable name.

print(f"With VAT: {charge * 1.2}")

The :.2f format specifier

That last line gives you 14.399999999999999. Nobody wants that on a till receipt. You want two decimal places.

Inside the braces, after the value, add a colon and a formatting instruction.

print(f"With VAT: {charge * 1.2:.2f}")

Output:

With VAT: 14.40

Now break that piece {charge * 1.2:.2f} into its parts.

PartMeaning
charge * 1.2the value you want shown
:"formatting instructions follow"
.2two digits after the decimal point
ffixed-point — show it as a plain decimal number
In plain english

Read the whole thing as: value, colon, then how to dress it.

Trap · the two colons

The colon in {fee:.2f} is not the same colon as the one after if.

The block colon means "an indented block follows me". This one lives inside braces inside a string, and means "here come the formatting rules". Same character on the keyboard, completely unrelated job.

Do not let the collision throw you. Ask which situation you are in: opening a block, or dressing a value.

Money, written out in full:

charge = 12
print(f"Total: £{charge:.2f}")     # Total: £12.00

The £ sits outside the braces, because it is literal text rather than a value to be looked up.

Two more you will meet before long:

pct = 0.157
print(f"Rate: {pct:.1%}")          # Rate: 15.7%

count = 1250000
print(f"Count: {count:,}")         # Count: 1,250,000
05

One fully worked scenario

Read this end to end before attempting any drill. This is what a finished sheet actually looks like.

Situation · the vet's out-of-hours desk

A veterinary practice charges a consultation fee. During normal hours the fee depends on the animal: £35 for a small animal, £55 for a large animal. Out of hours the fees are higher: £70 small, £110 large.

Separately, clients on the practice's care plan get 20% off whatever consultation fee applies to them. Clients not on the plan pay the full amount.

The receptionist, Ruth, needs one final amount to read out to the client.

Move 1 · Scene

At the end, one consultation fee in pounds must exist.

  • Part A — work out the basic consultation fee.
  • Part B — apply the care plan discount, if any.

B cannot start until A has produced a fee. Sequence confirmed.

Branch count for Part A: 2 × 2 = four possible basic fees.

Part B is a separate two-state decision applied afterwards. It does not multiply into Part A — it does not make eight routes. That distinction is the whole reason Part B sits outside.

Move 4 · Order

  • Outer question — is it out of hours? Because until you know that, the animal size tells you nothing. Two complete fee lists exist and you must open the right one before anything else is meaningful.
  • Inner question — small or large animal? Only meaningful once a fee list has been chosen.
  • Later, for everyone — is the client on the care plan? Asked once, at the far-left margin, after all four routes have rejoined.

Every route fills the carrier? Yes — all four end in an assignment to consultation_fee.

Trap

Why not put the care plan check inside each of the four routes?

Because that writes the same discount logic four times, which is four chances to mistype it and four places to edit when the rate changes. Wait for the rejoin. Ask once.

The code

# --- Facts ---
is_out_of_hours = True
animal_size = "large"
on_care_plan = True


# --- Part A: find the basic consultation fee ---
# Outer question: which fee list applies?
if is_out_of_hours:

    # Inner question: which animal, within the out-of-hours list?
    if animal_size == "small":
        consultation_fee = 70
    else:
        consultation_fee = 110

else:

    # Inner question: which animal, within the normal-hours list?
    if animal_size == "small":
        consultation_fee = 35
    else:
        consultation_fee = 55


# All four routes have now rejoined.
# consultation_fee holds exactly one value.


# --- Part B: apply the care plan discount ---
# Far-left margin: this applies to everyone, whatever route they took.
if on_care_plan:
    consultation_fee = consultation_fee * 0.80


# --- Output ---
print(f"Consultation fee: £{consultation_fee:.2f}")

Output:

Consultation fee: £88.00
Things to notice before you move on

There are six colons in that code, and every one of them sits on a line that opens a block. The else lines have colons despite asking nothing.

Part B is back at the far-left margin. That leftward return is the code saying out loud: the fee investigation is over.

consultation_fee is assigned in four places and read in two. One box, many routes into it.

The output needed :.2f, because 110 × 0.80 gives 88.0 — and a receipt should say 88.00.

06

The drills

Twenty scenarios. For each one: sheet first, code second.

Do not skip the sheet even when the answer feels obvious. The obvious ones are where the habit gets built cheaply — and cheap repetitions are the ones you can afford lots of.

Work through them in order. The structure changes deliberately as you go.

Tier 1 · Two facts, one nest

Drills 1–6. The same skeleton as the worked example, in a different world every time.

01The ferry

A ferry charges per vehicle. On weekdays: £25 for a car, £45 for a van. At weekends: £35 for a car, £60 for a van. Vehicles carrying a blue badge holder get £10 off the fare. Print the final fare.

02The climbing wall

Entry costs £14 for an adult and £8 for a child during peak hours. Off-peak it is £10 adult, £6 child. Anyone bringing their own harness gets £2 off. Print what they pay.

03The print shop

A print shop charges by paper size and finish. Standard finish: £0.15 per page for A4, £0.30 for A3. Premium finish: £0.40 for A4, £0.75 for A3. The customer states how many pages. Orders over 500 pages get 10% off the total. Print the total cost.

04The car park

For stays under 3 hours: £4 surface, £7 multi-storey. For stays of 3 hours or more: £9 surface, £15 multi-storey. Residents with a permit pay half. Print the charge.

Watch the boundary. Where exactly does "under 3 hours" end, and which side does 3.0 sit on?

05The kennels

Boarding costs £22 per night for a small dog and £30 for a large dog in the standard block. In the heated block it is £35 and £45. The owner states the number of nights. Bookings of 7 nights or more get 15% off. Print the total.

06The exam entry

An entry fee is £48 for a first attempt and £62 for a resit, if entering before the deadline. After the deadline both rise by a flat £25 late fee. Candidates receiving financial support pay nothing at all. Print the fee.

Careful — the late fee is added, not a separate price list. Does that change what your outer question should be? Justify your answer on the sheet.

Tier 2 · More facts, noise, wider state counts

Drills 7–13. Now the sheet has to do real work before the code can.

07The bike hire

Hybrid bikes: £8 up to 2 hours, £14 up to 6 hours, £20 for a full day. Electric bikes: £15, £25, £38 for the same durations. Members get 20% off. The shop is on Mill Street and the customer's name is Aisha. Print the charge.

Two of the facts in that paragraph are noise. Identify them explicitly on the sheet rather than just ignoring them.

08The pottery kiln

Earthenware: £5 small, £9 medium, £16 large. Stoneware: £8, £14, £24. Porcelain: £12, £20, £34. Studio members get their first firing of the month free; everyone else pays full price. Print the cost.

Three states in the outer question now, not two. What does that do to your if / elif / else?

09The allotment

Full plots: £95 a year standard, £60 concession. Half plots: £55 / £35. Quarter plots: £30 / £20. Tenants on the committee get £15 off. Print the annual rent.

Is plot size the outer question, or is tenant status? Try it both ways on the sheet and argue for one.

10The bakery order

Sponge cakes: £28 for 6 inch, £40 for 8 inch, £58 for 10 inch. Fruit cakes: £35, £52, £74. A written message costs £6. A sugar-flower decoration costs £18. A customer may add neither, either, or both. Print the total.

Two independent add-ons after the rejoin, not one. How does that change the shape of Part B?

11The council tip

Rubble: £12 per load for householders, £45 for traders. Green waste: free for householders, £20 for traders. Plasterboard: £25 / £60. Anyone arriving in a van is charged the trader rate regardless of who they actually are. Print the charge.

That last sentence is doing something sneaky to your fact list. Deal with it on the sheet before you touch any code.

12The theatre seat

Evening performance: stalls £45, circle £35, balcony £22. For a matinee, all prices drop by a third. Under-16s and over-65s pay 50% of whatever price applies to them. Print the ticket price.

Two separate conditions lead to the same concession. What does that suggest about how you write the test?

13The plant nursery

Priced by pot size: 9cm £3.50, 1 litre £6, 3 litre £11, 10 litre £28. Trade customers get 30% off. Retail customers spending £50 or more get 10% off. Nobody gets both. Print the price for a single plant.

Then state on your sheet what would change if the customer bought several plants rather than one.

Tier 3 · Structure that breaks the pattern

Drills 14–20. The skeleton still applies. It just stops looking like it does.

14The blood donation screener

A donor is eligible if they are aged 17 to 65, weigh at least 50 kg, and have not donated in the last 12 weeks. If they are outside the age range, print that specific reason. If underweight, print that reason. If they donated too recently, print how many more weeks they must wait. If everything passes, print that they are eligible.

Your carrier here is not a number. What is it? And what is the correct order of gates?

15The parcel locker

Lockers are assigned by parcel size — small to size S, medium to M, large to L. If the recipient requested ground level and a ground-level locker of the right size is free, assign that. If not, assign any locker of the right size. If no locker of the right size is free at all, the parcel goes to the collection desk. Print the assignment.

Two carriers, or one? Argue it on the sheet before you decide.

16The tiered water bill

The first 20 cubic metres cost £1.10 each. The next 30 cost £1.80 each. Everything above 50 costs £2.60 each. A household on the social tariff has the total reduced by 25%. Print the bill.

This one is not "pick a band" — it is "accumulate across bands". Your carrier starts at zero and grows. Say so on the sheet before you write anything at all.

17The overdue account

Under 30 days late: a reminder email, whatever the value. 30 to 89 days: email for accounts under £500, phone call for £500 or more. 90 days or more: phone call under £500, referral to collections for £500 or more. Accounts flagged as being in hardship never go to collections — they get a phone call instead. Print the action.

The hardship rule overrides a decision made earlier. Where does that check go — before, inside, or after? Justify it.

18The school trip roster

A list of pupils, each with a name, an age, whether a consent form is returned, and whether they have paid. Loop the list. Print each pupil's status, count how many are cleared to travel (form returned and paid), count how many are blocked, and print the two counts at the end.

Carriers that accumulate inside a loop. Name them on the sheet, and say what they hold before the loop starts.

19The takeaway delivery fee

Delivery is free on orders of £30 or more within 3 miles. Between 3 and 6 miles it is free on orders of £45 or more. Beyond 6 miles delivery is not offered. Where delivery is not free, it costs £2.50 within 3 miles and £4.50 between 3 and 6. Orders under £12 are not accepted at all. Print either the fee or a refusal message.

Which gate disqualifies fastest? Put that one first and watch how much of the rest collapses.

20The grading moderator

A cohort of results, each with a raw mark and whether the pupil had approved extra time. Bands: under 40 fail, 40–54 pass, 55–69 merit, 70 and above distinction. Any pupil with approved extra time whose mark falls within 2 of a band boundary is lifted to the higher band. Loop the cohort, print each pupil's final band, and print how many were lifted.

Everything in this pack, at once. Take your time on the sheet — the code will be far shorter than you fear, if the sheet is right.

07

After each drill

Three questions, every time, thirty seconds. This is where the retention actually happens — not during the drill, but here.

Question one

Which move did I want to skip?

That is your weak move. Name it out loud. It will be the same one for a while, and then one day it won't be.

Question two

Did my branch count from Move 2 match the number of branches in my code?

If not — where did the mismatch come from? A miscount on the sheet, or a route you forgot to write?

Question three

Would I get this right cold in a fortnight?

If no, mark the drill and come back to it in three days.

08

How to use this pack

Trap

Do not do twenty in a day. You will get faster and remember less — which is exactly the failure mode you are here to fix.

  • Three to four drills per session, spread across days.
  • Re-do drill 1 after drill 8. Then again after drill 15. Cold, from the scenario text, without looking at your old sheet.
  • That spacing is what converts it from something you did into something you know. It is uncomfortable on purpose.
Resolution

When the sheet starts to feel like an annoying formality, that is not a sign to stop.

That is the target arriving.

The point is never the ferry, the vet, or the pottery kiln. The point is that by drill 20 you can read a scenario you have never seen before and know, without effort, that your first job is to finish the sentence:

At the end, one ______ must exist.
